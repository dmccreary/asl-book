## 1. Understanding Communication Modes

When we think of language, we often think of speaking and listening. However, human language is much broader. Communication can be divided into different channels, or **modes**, depending on how information is sent and received. Understanding these modes helps us see how American Sign Language is structured.

### Visual Communication vs. Auditory-Oral Communication

For hearing individuals, the primary mode of language is **Auditory-Oral Communication**. This system uses sound waves produced by the mouth (oral) and received by the ears (auditory). In contrast, **Visual Communication** relies on sight. It is a broad category that includes anything we see to gain information—from traffic signs and drawings to written words and body language.

American Sign Language (ASL) operates entirely within the visual mode. Instead of sound, it uses a **Visual-Gestural** channel. The signer produces messages using body movement, hands, and facial expressions, and the receiver processes this information through their eyes.

### Manual Communication

Within visual communication, we find **Manual Communication**. This refers specifically to using the hands, arms, and body to transmit messages. Manual communication has a long history, appearing in gestural systems, historical monastic sign codes, and modern sign languages. It is the physical production mechanism of sign language, contrasting with the vocal tract used in spoken languages.

## 2. What is American Sign Language?

**American Sign Language (ASL)** is a complete, natural language that is the primary language of many Deaf people in the United States and parts of Canada. 

It is important to understand what ASL is **not**:
* **It is not English on the hands.** ASL has its own unique grammar, sentence structure, rules, and vocabulary that are completely separate from English.
* **It is not universal.** Deaf people in different countries use different sign languages. For example, British Sign Language (BSL) and French Sign Language (LSF) are entirely different languages from ASL.
* **It is not just simple gestures.** Gestures are informal body movements we use to emphasize speech. ASL signs are formal, structured vocabulary words with precise rules.

As a natural language, ASL has evolved over hundreds of years. It features a rich literature, including poetry, storytelling, and theatrical performances, all designed for visual expression.

## 3. The Deaf Community and Deaf Culture

To learn ASL is to learn about the people who speak it. The language and its speakers are deeply intertwined.

### The Deaf Community

The **Deaf Community** is a diverse group of individuals who share a common linguistic and cultural bond. This community is not defined simply by a medical diagnosis of hearing loss. Instead, it is a social group that includes:
* Deaf individuals who use ASL to communicate.
* Hard-of-hearing individuals who identify with the community.
* Hearing family members, friends, interpreters, and educators who participate in Deaf culture.

Within the community, the word "Deaf" is spelled with a capital **"D"** when referring to the cultural and social group (Deaf Culture), and with a lowercase **"d"** when referring to the physiological condition of not hearing (deafness).

### Deaf Culture

**Deaf Culture** consists of the unique values, social beliefs, traditions, and artistic expressions shared by the Deaf community. Some core values of Deaf Culture include:
* **Visual Alert Systems**: Using flashing lights for doorbells, smoke detectors, and baby monitors instead of sound alarms.
* **Collectivism**: A strong sense of group loyalty, community support, and shared history.
* **Directness**: Clear, honest, and direct communication is highly valued.
* **Storytelling**: Visual storytelling and sign play are celebrated traditions.

## 4. The Five Parameters of ASL

Just like spoken words are made of individual sounds (phonemes), ASL signs are built from five distinct physical components called **parameters**. If you change even one parameter, you change the entire meaning of the sign—or turn it into a nonsense gesture.

The five parameters of ASL are:
1. **Handshape**: The configuration of your hand (e.g., flat, fist, index finger pointed).
2. **Palm Orientation**: The direction your palm faces (e.g., forward, backward, left, right).
3. **Location**: The physical spot on or near your body where the sign is made.
4. **Movement**: The action or path your hands take (e.g., straight line, circle, wiggling, tapping).
5. **Facial Expression (Non-Manual Signals)**: Grammatical facial movements and body shifts.

Let's study these parameters in detail.

| Parameter | Key Concept | Examples in Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Handshape** | The shape of the hand(s). | Using a flat "B" hand vs. an index "1" hand. |
| **Palm Orientation** | The direction the palm faces. | Palm facing inward toward you vs. outward toward the receiver. |
| **Location** | Where the sign is physically anchored. | Making a sign on your forehead vs. your chin or in neutral space. |
| **Movement** | The path, speed, or repetition of the hands. | Moving hands in a circular path vs. a sharp downward stroke. |
| **Facial Expression** | Non-manual signals that convey grammar. | Raising eyebrows for a question vs. furrowing them. |

## 5. Deconstructing the Parameters

Let's examine how each of the five parameters contributes to the structure and meaning of ASL signs.

### Handshape

The **Handshape** is the shape your hand makes. Many ASL handshapes are based on the letters of the fingerspelled alphabet or numbers (which we will learn in Chapter 2).

For example, compare the signs for **MY** and **I/ME**:
* To sign **MY**, you use a flat, open handshape (often called the "B" handshape) pressed against your chest.
* To sign **I/ME**, you use an index finger handshape (the "1" handshape) pointing to your chest.
The *only* difference between these two signs is the shape of your hand.

### Palm Orientation

**Palm Orientation** refers to which way your palm is facing. Your palm can face inward (toward yourself), outward (toward the receiver), up, down, left, or right.

For example, look at the signs for **CLEAN** and **PAPER**:
* For both signs, you place your dominant hand flat on top of your non-dominant hand.
* For **CLEAN**, your palm slides smoothly across.
* For **PAPER**, the palms brush against each other in a repeating backward motion.
Changing palm orientation changes the meaning entirely!

!!! mascot-tip "Fingerspelling Tip"
    ![Mimi tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    When fingerspelling or signing numbers, keep your palm facing **outward** toward the person you are communicating with. Turning your palm inward makes it hard for others to read your signs!

### Location

**Location** is the physical position of the sign. Signs can be made on the forehead, face, chin, neck, shoulders, chest, or in the open space in front of your body (called **neutral space**).

For example, look at the gender signs in ASL:
* Signs for male family members are made near the **forehead** (temple area).
* Signs for female family members are made near the **chin** (jawline area).
* **FATHER** is signed by tapping the thumb of an open "5" hand on the forehead.
* **MOTHER** is signed by tapping the thumb of an open "5" hand on the chin.
Everything about these signs is identical except for their location!

### Movement

**Movement** is the action of your hands. Signs can move in straight lines, arcs, circles, wiggles, waves, or repeating taps. Movement can be fast or slow, showing intensity or duration.

For example, look at nouns and verbs:
* In ASL, nouns (objects) often have a **double, smaller movement**.
* Verbs (actions) often have a **single, larger movement**.
* **CHAIR** (noun) is signed with two quick, small downward taps of the fingers.
* **SIT** (verb) is signed with a single, larger downward movement.

### Facial Expression (Non-Manual Signals)

**Facial Expression**, also called **Non-Manual Signals (NMS)**, refers to movements of your face and body that accompany your signs. This is not just decoration—it is the literal **grammar** of ASL!

Facial expressions carry meaning:
* **Yes/No Questions**: Raise your eyebrows and tilt your head forward.
* **WH-Questions (Who, What, When, Where, Why)**: Furrow (lower) your eyebrows and tilt your head forward.
* **Negation (saying "no" or "not")**: Shake your head from side to side while signing.

Without facial expressions, your signing is like speaking in a flat, expressionless computer voice!

!!! mascot-warning "Common Mistake"
    ![Mimi warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Don't look down at your hands while signing! Keep your eyes on the face of the person you are communicating with, and look them in the eyes. In Deaf culture, eye contact is crucial for clear communication.

## 6. How Parameter Shifts Change Meaning

Because ASL signs are constructed from these parameters, changing just one parameter alters the sign's meaning. Linguists call this a **parameter shift**. It is the ASL equivalent of changing a single sound in a spoken word (like changing the "c" in "cat" to "h" to make "hat").

## 8. Summary and Key Takeaways

Visual languages are just as rich and structured as spoken languages. By understanding how ASL is constructed, you are ready to begin fingerspelling, signing numbers, and building conversations.

### Key Takeaways
* **Language Mode**: ASL is a visual-gestural language, not a spoken one. It uses manual production (hands, body) and visual reception (eyes).
* **ASL Identity**: ASL has its own grammar and structure completely separate from English.
* **The Deaf Community**: Defined culturally and linguistically (Deaf with a capital "D"), sharing unique cultural values.
* **The 5 Parameters**: Handshape, Palm Orientation, Location, Movement, and Facial Expression are the contrastive units of any sign.
* **Facial Grammar**: Non-manual signals (like raising or lowering eyebrows) carry the actual grammar and sentence structure of ASL.
